# Scientific Papers Made Easy — Summary of Recommendations

**Stuart West & Lindsay Turnbull**, Oxford University Press (2023)
*How to Write With Clarity and Impact in the Life Sciences*

> **Scope note:** This summary covers only the front matter, Chapter 1 "Writing as an Essential Research Skill", and Chapter 2 "Core Skills". Chapter 2 promises ten tips; nine are summarised here. Chapters 3–11 (Methods, Results, Figures, Introductions, Discussions, Abstracts, Titles, Cover Letters, Writing and Editing) are not included.

The recommendations below are for writing a scientific paper. Read them as advice to a model writing a proposal (for a plan or a feature) that a human will review, using this mapping:

| In the book | Here |
|---|---|
| the paper | the proposal document |
| the scientist writing the paper | the model drafting the proposal |
| the reviewer | the human, in the interactive discussion over the first few drafts |
| the readers | the human who requested the proposal, and anyone who returns to this work later |

Two consequences of that mapping are worth stating outright:

- **The reviewer's confusion is evidence about the writing, not about the reviewer.** A question asked during the discussion marks a part of the document that was not clear enough, so suggest a fix to the document rather than only answering the question.
- **The tips apply to every revision, not just the first draft.** An edit made mid-discussion is held to the same rules as the text it replaces.

---

## 1. The Guiding Principle: The Reader Comes First

The book's single organising idea is that writing exists to serve the reader, not the writer. Writers often write to sound scientific or to satisfy themselves, when their actual job is to make the content as easy to absorb as possible. The authors argue that good writing cannot rescue bad science, but bad writing can sink good science — research is a chain of three links (finding a problem, solving it, communicating the result), and it is only as strong as the weakest one.

- **Picture a realistic reader, not an ideal one.**
  - Assume your reader is time-limited, stressed, and easily bored, competing against email, other papers, and everything else on the internet.
  - Assume they are looking for an excuse to stop reading — your job is to remove those excuses.
  - Remember that, apart from reviewers, nobody is obliged to read your paper at all.
- **Treat reviewers as representative readers.**
  - A reviewer was chosen by an editor as someone qualified to assess your work, so if they find the paper confusing, that is evidence about your writing rather than about their competence.
  - Confused reviewers may simply reject the paper rather than search for its hidden merits.
- **Treat writing as a skill that must be learnt and practised.**
  - Budget real time for it, as you would for a new lab technique or a programming language.
  - Do not treat writing as a quick task tacked on after "the real work" is done.
  - Expect to keep improving throughout your career.

## 2. Recommended Order of Writing

Although a finished paper is read in a fixed order, the authors recommend writing the sections in the order that is easiest, starting with the parts that require the least interpretation.

For example, for a scientific paper that would be:
- **Start with the Methods.** You are simply describing what you did, so there is no interpretive burden.
- **Write the Results next.** They follow naturally from the Methods.
- **Then the Introduction and Discussion.** These are harder, because they need context, links to other published work, and genuine interpretation.
- **Write the Abstract last**, despite it appearing first in the finished paper. Only once everything else is settled do you know what the story of the paper actually is.
- **Finish with the Title, the cover letter, and a full editing pass.**

For a proposal, the same idea applies: draft the parts that need the least interpretation first (files changed, detailed implementation), and write the description and overview last. The minimalism recommendation in [§3.3](#33-keep-it-to-essentials) matters most here.

## 3. Four General Principles

These four principles apply to every section of a paper.

### 3.1 Keep it simple

New writers often adopt a style they think makes them sound like a scientist — technical vocabulary, abbreviations, and phrasing they would never use in conversation. This makes the paper harder to read, not more credible.

- Aim for clear and concise rather than clever or impressive.
- Accept that plain English is often *harder* to write than technical language, and do it anyway.
- Expect it to become more natural with practice.

### 3.2 Assume nothing

You have thought about your work daily for months or years, so you are, paradoxically, the worst-placed person to judge what a newcomer needs explained. Psychologists and economists call this the "curse of knowledge" — the tendency to unconsciously assume that others share the background knowledge you have.

- Assume your reader is scientifically literate but has very little specialist knowledge of your specific topic.
- Remember that even an expert in your field does not know what *you* did or why you did it.
- Walk through every crucial step in order, taking the reader with you.
- Err toward over-explaining: a paper is far more likely to fail from assuming too much than from being too basic.
- Be especially careful in work that spans disciplines, where readers arrive with entirely different backgrounds.

### 3.3 Keep it to essentials

The authors recommend stripping writing down to the bare minimum needed to do the job — what they call minimalism. A paper is not an opportunity to demonstrate breadth of knowledge or to air opinions on adjacent topics.

- Cut digressions that are not necessary to understand the study.
- Include everything needed for comprehension, but nothing beyond that.
- Accept that editing down to "just enough" is harder work than writing something long.
- Remember that readers absorb only a small fraction of what you write, so removing distractions increases the chance your main message survives.
- Note the practical payoff: people are more likely to start *and* finish shorter papers.

### 3.4 Tell your story

Good scientific writing has a narrative — a term borrowed from creative writing, where "narrative flow" means each part connects smoothly to the next so the reader is carried along rather than left to reassemble the pieces.

- Make clear why the topic matters, what you found, and why that finding matters.
- Link the parts explicitly so one section leads into the next.
- Aim for a reader who is gently pulled forward, curious about what comes next, rather than confused or frustrated.

## 4. Top Tips

Nine of the book's ten specific tips appear in this extract. They are presented as tips rather than rules — the authors are explicit that experienced writers break these conventions deliberately, and that these are aimed at people starting out or trying to improve.

### 4.1 Write as you speak

If you would not say a word out loud to a friend, do not put it in your paper. The example given is "whilst": a word almost nobody uses in speech.

- Explain the science the way you would explain it in conversation.
- Use the "read it aloud" test — if you sound like a robot, rewrite it.
- Example rewrite: "We assigned birds to two experimental groups, where we manipulated the nutritional resources to different levels" becomes "We divided the birds into two groups, which received small or large amounts of food."

### 4.2 Write short sentences that make only one point

Human readers hold a whole sentence in their head while parsing it, so an overloaded sentence forces re-reading.

- Put one idea per sentence.
- Break long sentences into two or more short ones.
- Use a rough alarm bell: if a sentence runs longer than two lines in a word processor, examine whether it should be split.
- Read it aloud — overloading becomes obvious immediately.
- Do not overcorrect: a string of very short sentences is its own problem.

### 4.3 Avoid jargon

Jargon — specialist vocabulary understood only by a narrow group — is sometimes used to sound clever or scientific, but it drives readers away. The authors cite a study finding that papers containing more specialist terminology are cited less often.

- Judge jargon relative to your intended audience: in fields such as immunology, some technical terms are unavoidable, so the target becomes avoiding terms that even immunologists would not broadly recognise.
- The general test is to cut terms that only a narrow set of specialists would know.
- When you do need a technical term to connect your work to existing literature, write the plain-English version first and attach the term in brackets afterwards, e.g. describe the situation, then label it ("social dilemmas").
- Example rewrite: "In microbial systems, intraspecific competition is induced by stressful conditions such as resource or space limitation" becomes "Bacteria compete for space and resources."
- Be warned that this is easy to slip into — every "bad" example in the book was taken from a real published paper.

### 4.4 Write cause before effect

Most scientific sentences contain something that happened and something that resulted. Putting the cause first means the explanation arrives before the thing it explains, so the reader never has to backtrack.

- Order each sentence: what you did or what varied, then what followed.
- Example rewrite: "More matings with females are obtained by male peacocks with larger tails" becomes "Male peacocks with larger tails mated with more females."
- Example rewrite: "Females laid more eggs when they were larger" becomes "Larger females laid more eggs."

### 4.5 Make one major point per paragraph

Paragraphs exist to break text into units the reader can grasp. A paragraph carrying two major points buries both.

- Restrict each paragraph to a single main point so it stands out.
- Aim for roughly three to eight sentences as a typical length.

### 4.6 Use the first sentence of a paragraph to summarise it

Put the paragraph's main point up front, then use the rest of the paragraph for detail — explanation, evidence, statistics.

- Lead with the key point so the reader knows where the paragraph is going and feels at ease.
- Test yourself: read only the first sentences of every paragraph in a section or the whole paper, and check that someone reading just those would come away with a reasonable understanding. The answer should be yes.
- Design for skimmers and for readers whose concentration lapses mid-paragraph — they should still collect the main points.

### 4.7 Be precise and consistent

Scientific progress depends on reliable communication between scientists, so precision outranks elegant variation.

- Fix your terms and reuse them exactly. If you defined your 2 m × 2 m areas as "plots", never later call them "patches".
- Sacrifice variety of language when variety would cost precision.
- Actively check for this — slipping into synonyms across different parts of a paper happens surprisingly easily.
- Stay consistent with the wider literature too: using an established term in your own idiosyncratic way spreads confusion and holds the field back.

### 4.8 Follow the section headings

Each heading is a clue about what belongs beneath it, and the standard sections already sit in a logical order: why the study was needed (Introduction), what you did (Methods), what you found (Results), what it means (Discussion).

- Put content where its heading says it goes — for instance, do not open a Discussion with a lengthy account of why the work was needed, which belongs in the Introduction.
- Treat misplaced content as a symptom: either you are repeating yourself, or the proper section was not done well enough.
- Recognise that some repetition between sections is useful. Deliberate linking and summarising helps the reader; the book flags where this is worthwhile in later chapters.

### 4.9 Plan before you write

The authors explicitly reject the common advice to beat a blank page by "just getting something down" and recommend the opposite.

- Plan each section as thoroughly as you can before writing any prose.
- Begin with bullet points of what must be covered — for the Introduction, the reasons the paper is needed; for the Methods, the essential steps of your protocol.
- Plan on paper rather than on screen, so you can shuffle the points around.
- Link and order the bullets into a flow diagram or mind map before drafting.
